Where a Death Valley retreat goes

Canyons, a pass, the salt flats and the dark.

The park is bigger than some states. We use a handful of places, each chosen because of what it does to a team, with guided vehicles, water planning and a satellite link throughout.

Titus Canyon

A one-way dirt road that drops from the Nevada side through a narrow canyon to the valley floor. High-clearance vehicles, walls closing in, and no way to turn back: a good metaphor for a team that has been circling a decision.

Lippincott Pass

A steep, rough 4x4 route toward the Racetrack, signed for high-clearance vehicles only. The kind of terrain where a team has to communicate or stop moving. It is where our auto retailer team spent two days stranded together.

Badwater Basin

282 feet below sea level, the lowest point in North America. Salt flats to the horizon and a natural setting for the “where are we really?” conversation.

The night sky

The park is a Gold Tier International Dark Sky Park, the highest rating. Even at Furnace Creek, with the resort’s own lighting, the sky rates Bortle Class 2; a short drive out it gets darker still. An evening session under that sky changes the tone of what people are willing to say.

Planning a retreat in Death Valley

The practical details.

When to go

November to March only. Summer is among the hottest on Earth and not suitable for a retreat.

Getting there

Las Vegas (LAS) is about 120 miles, roughly 2 to 2.5 hours by road to Furnace Creek. Los Angeles is about 4.5 hours.

How long

Two to three days. The landscape does its work fast.

Where you stay

We use the historic Furnace Creek Inn (now The Inn at Death Valley), opened in 1927, so the hard day ends with comfort and a long table.
